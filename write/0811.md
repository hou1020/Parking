## Provisional title

**Evaluating the Geographic Transfer of a US-Trained Surface-Parking Segmentation Model: Evidence from Leeds**

## One-sentence version

This dissertation tests whether a US-trained parking-lot segmentation model can be used to measure off-street surface parking in Leeds, and argues that the transferred map is useful for city-scale estimates after local validation, but not for direct area measurement or site-level interpretation.

## Central dissertation argument

The project begins from a practical data gap: UK cities do not have a consistent spatial dataset of off-street surface parking, even though parking is relevant to debates about land use, density and redevelopment pressure. A trained remote-sensing model could help fill that gap, but only if its output is validated in the UK context.

The dissertation therefore does three things in sequence:

1. It tests the model against a manually labelled reference dataset for 100 km² of Leeds.
2. It explains the main sources of error rather than reporting accuracy alone.
3. It uses the measured reliability to judge what the resulting map can and cannot support.

The main expected conclusion is that the model transfers unevenly. It has high recall but low precision, meaning that it finds much of the true parking area but substantially over-predicts total area. The raw output is therefore not reliable as a direct measurement of parking extent. However, because the over-prediction is measurable and fairly stable at aggregate scale, the model can still support city- or district-scale estimates after local calibration.

In short: the dissertation is not asking only whether the model is accurate. It is asking what uses the model output can reasonably support.

## Research questions

| RQ | Question | Role in the dissertation |
|---|---|---|
| RQ1 | How accurate is a US-trained surface-parking segmentation model on UK aerial imagery, and does accuracy vary systematically within the city? | Establishes whether transfer works at all, and whether performance varies across Leeds |
| RQ2 | What systematic errors does the model make, and how much error is removed or created by post-processing? | Explains why the headline accuracy values look the way they do |
| RQ3 | Under the measured reliability, how much central-city land is surface parking, and where is it concentrated? | Uses the validation results to make a cautious land-area estimate |

## Shape of the final dissertation

The dissertation will be organised around a simple progression:

**Problem -> model -> local validation -> error mechanisms -> calibrated use -> limits.**

## Chapter 1: Introduction

**Purpose:** explain why the project matters, what gap it addresses, and what the dissertation will test.

The introduction will open with parking as an urban land-use issue rather than as a machine-learning problem. UK densification debates require evidence about low-intensity land uses, but there is no consistent national or local spatial dataset of off-street surface parking. OpenStreetMap is useful but incomplete, and existing parking evidence is weighted towards US case studies.

The chapter will then introduce the opportunity and risk of using a trained segmentation model. Qiam, Devunuri and Lehe's US-trained model provides a practical starting point, but a model trained in one national imagery and urban-form context cannot be assumed to transfer to a UK city. This leads to the three research questions.

**What this chapter must establish**

- The dissertation is about both parking as land and model transfer.
- A one-city validation study is a deliberate design choice: wider comparison would be premature before reliability is known.
- The study does not make site-level redevelopment recommendations.

**Likely structure**

1. Parking as an under-measured urban land use.
2. The UK spatial data gap.
3. Remote sensing as a possible solution.
4. Why transferability must be tested.
5. Research questions and chapter outline.

## Chapter 2: Background

**Purpose:** build the conceptual basis for the study and show where the research gap sits.

This chapter will connect four bodies of work that are usually separate: parking and land-use debates, remote-sensing segmentation, model transfer/domain shift, and the limitations of volunteered or administrative reference data.

The most important part of the chapter will be the domain-transfer framing. The dissertation should not just say "the model was trained in the US and tested in the UK"; it should explain what differences might matter. These include smaller or more irregular car parks, different surface materials, more unmarked parking, longer shadows at the latitude of Leeds, different vehicle mixes, and the lack of a NIR band in the UK imagery used here.

These differences will be written as expectations that the results chapter can test. For example, if unmarked parking is expected to be difficult, the sampled false negatives can show whether lack of markings is actually the main problem. Current results suggest that irregular layout matters more than absence of markings, which is a useful example of the data refining the initial expectation.

**Likely structure**

1. Parking as urban land.
2. Existing evidence on parking extent and its US bias.
3. Semantic segmentation of parking from aerial imagery.
4. The Qiam, Devunuri and Lehe model and its annotation definition.
5. Domain shift between US training data and UK application.
6. Why OSM cannot be treated as ground truth.
7. Research gap and contribution.

**What this chapter must land**

The gap is not simply "nobody has mapped Leeds parking". The gap is that a scalable model exists, but its transferred output has not been tested in a way that says what planning or urban-analysis tasks it can support.

## Chapter 3: Methodology

**Purpose:** make the validation design reproducible and justify the definitions used in the results.

This chapter is the most settled part of the dissertation. It will describe the Leeds study area, the imagery, the model pipeline, the manual reference data, the accuracy metrics, and the error analysis.

The manual labelling rules are central. The reference data follows the model's target definition as closely as possible: off-street surface parking, visible from above, including visible rooftop parking, excluding on-street parking and single-house driveways. This matters because the model should be assessed against the category it was trained to recognise, not against a newly invented definition.

The validation will use area-based metrics:

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| TP | model area overlapping the manual reference |
| FP | model area outside the manual reference |
| FN | manual reference area missed by the model |
| Precision | TP / model area |
| Recall | TP / manual area |
| IoU | TP / union of model and manual areas |

The chapter will also explain the two-track error analysis:

- automated attribution, using buffers, OSM/OS layers and object-level coverage;
- stratified image-chip sampling, used where automated attribution cannot explain the error.

**Likely structure**

1. Study area and datasets.
2. Manual annotation protocol.
3. Model and post-processing pipeline.
4. Validation metrics and aggregation.
5. False-positive attribution.
6. False-negative attribution.
7. Stratified sampling of unexplained cases.
8. Ablation tests for post-processing.
9. Robustness checks: imagery consistency, co-registration, OSM timestamps and calibration hold-out.

**What this chapter must be careful about**

- State the reference area convention: the labelled polygons do not overlap each other, and the 3.2677 -> 3.2597 km2 difference is clipping at the study-area boundary, not overlap removal.
- Explain the 5 m boundary band as a convention, with 2 m and 10 m reported for sensitivity.
- Distinguish "FP located on industrial/commercial land" from "this individual polygon is definitely an industrial yard".
- State the sampling frame limit for small fragments.
- Explain that the calibration factor is an area-ratio estimator, and that its value is tested by hold-out rather than asserted.

## Chapter 4: Results

**Purpose:** answer the research questions with a selective set of results.

This chapter has the greatest risk of becoming too long. The plan is to organise it around the three RQs, but to present RQ2 in enough detail to explain the accuracy values from RQ1.

### 4.1 Overall accuracy and transferability

The post-processed model output has:

| Metric | Value |
|---|---:|
| Precision | 0.571 |
| Recall | 0.854 |
| IoU | 0.520 |
| Manual area | 3.26 km² |
| Modelled area | 4.88 km² |
| Over-prediction | 1.50x |

The result is asymmetric: the model finds most parking area, but predicts too much area overall. This will be the headline answer to RQ1.

### 4.2 False positives

Total false-positive area is 2.09 km², which is why precision is low. The section will separate this into boundary effects, attributable confusions, definitional differences and a genuinely unexplained residual.

**Boundary dilation.** The first split is between area lying close to a real car park, where the model has found the lot but drawn it too large, and standalone detections elsewhere.

| Threshold | Boundary dilation | Standalone |
|---|---:|---:|
| 2 m | 17.3% | 82.7% |
| 5 m | 28.8% | 71.2% |
| 10 m | 36.3% | 63.7% |

Three thresholds are reported because 5 m is a working convention rather than a natural break. At the working threshold, roughly 29% of false-positive area is boundary imprecision rather than misdetection.

**Attribution of standalone false positives.** Standalone area is attributed against reference layers in two ways: an exclusive partition, in which each square metre is assigned once and shares sum to 100%; and an unordered diagnostic, in which each layer is intersected with all false positives independently.

| Layer | Exclusive (% of all FP) | Unordered overlap (% of all FP) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Boundary dilation (≤5 m) | 28.8 | — |
| Industrial / commercial land | 29.6 | 52.8 |
| Road-adjacent (+6 m) | 11.6 | 16.9 |
| OSM parking | 4.7 | 9.1 |
| Sports courts | 2.5 | 3.1 |
| Brownfield | 1.7 | 2.3 |
| Buildings | 0.0 | 0.0 |
| Unexplained | 21.0 | — |

Two points need to be made explicitly in the text. The two columns have different denominators and are not differences of one another: the unordered figures include the dilation band and may overlap each other. And the buildings row is zero only because building footprints have already been subtracted in post-processing; false positives on unmapped buildings fall into the unexplained residual.

The attribution is also positional. "False positive located on industrial land" is a statement about where the area falls, not a confirmation that each polygon is a storage yard.

**Sampled composition of the unexplained residual.** The 21% that no layer explains was characterised by stratified sampling of 70 image chips, which divides it into three quite different things:

| Group | Share of residual |
|---|---:|
| Genuine misdetection (grey hardstanding, goods yards, sports courts, unpaved ground, houses) | 44.5% |
| Definitional difference (private driveways 20.2%, on-street parking 14.7%) | 34.9% |
| Real parking the labelling missed | 17.2% |

The most useful result here is that over a third of the unexplained residual is real parking that the annotation rules deliberately exclude. That area is a disagreement about scope, not a failure of recognition, and treating it as model error understates the model.

The interpretive point of the section is that reference layers can explain where error falls but cannot be used to clean the map. Section 4.4 provides the evidence for the second half of that claim.

### 4.3 False negatives

Total false-negative area is 0.47 km², or 14.6% of labelled parking. The structure of the section mirrors 4.2, but the more informative split here is by car park rather than by fragment.

**Boundary erosion.** As with dilation, area lying immediately alongside a detection is a boundary effect rather than a missed lot.

| Threshold | Boundary erosion | Standalone |
|---|---:|---:|
| 2 m | 33.4% | 66.6% |
| 5 m | 54.1% | 45.9% |
| 10 m | 69.4% | 30.6% |

**Missed area by the state of the lot it belongs to.** A fragment-based definition conflates undetected car parks with the ragged edges of ones the model largely found, so lots are classified by how much of them the model covered.

| Class | Lots | Share of FN |
|---|---:|---:|
| Whole lot missed (≤10% covered) | 121 | 23.8% |
| Partly detected (10–70%) | 278 | 31.8% |
| Fringe of a well-detected lot (>70%) | 1,638 | 44.4% |

Nearly half of all missed area is the edge of car parks the model actually found. Only the first row is a detection failure in any useful sense.

**Where the whole-lot misses come from.** Attributing that first row is the most consequential result in the section.

| Cause | Share of whole-lot misses |
|---|---:|
| Removed by post-processing (the raw model had found it) | 31.9% |
| Genuinely not detected | 61.8% |
| Labelled as rooftop | 2.9% |
| Falling inside OSM building footprints | 3.4% |

Roughly a third of the car parks the model appears to have missed were in fact found by the model and then deleted by the pipeline. The genuine blind spot is at most 2.1% of labelled area, and sampling suggests part of even that is not parking in the imagery the model was given.

**What gets missed.** Detection is strongly size-dependent, and tracks annotator confidence.

| Lot size | Mean detection rate | Missed entirely |
|---|---:|---:|
| < 200 m² | 0.658 | 19.1% |
| > 5,000 m² | 0.864 | 1.8% |

| Annotator confidence | Mean detection rate | Missed entirely |
|---|---:|---:|
| 1 (uncertain) | 0.713 | 10.6% |
| 3 (clear) | 0.856 | 5.4% |

The confidence result is worth stating plainly: where a human annotator found the call difficult, the model did too. This is evidence that part of the measured error reflects genuine ambiguity in the target rather than model deficiency, and it also sets a ceiling on what a single-annotator reference can measure.

**Sampled composition of the genuine misses.** Forty-two chips characterise the residual:

| Category | Share |
|---|---:|
| Not parking in the Digimap imagery | 41.8% |
| Irregular layout | 23.3% |
| Obscured by shadow or canopy | 9.8% |
| Unusual surface | 9.2% |
| No cars present | 6.3% |
| Vans and lorries rather than cars | 5.2% |
| No markings | 3.7% |

This is where the section connects back to the domain-shift expectations of Chapter 2. Irregular layout was assigned to 11 of the 42 chips against one for absent markings, which revises the expectation stated in the background chapter: the model appears to depend less on painted bays than on an arrangement matching the bay-and-aisle template it learned. The n=1 estimate for markings is not a precise share, so this is a direction for future training data rather than a settled quantity.

### 4.4 Post-processing and ablation

The ablation results show that building and road removal slightly improve precision and IoU but reduce recall.

| Output | Precision | Recall | IoU |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Raw model | 0.528 | 0.894 | 0.497 |
| Post-processed model | 0.571 | 0.854 | 0.520 |

The rooftop case is the clearest example of the trade-off. The raw model detects visible rooftop parking well, but the building-removal step removes most of it. This shows that the pipeline itself creates a systematic blind spot.

### 4.5 Accuracy within the city

This section will test whether accuracy varies spatially. Current results suggest that distance from Leeds City Square is not the main driver. Precision is better explained by parking abundance in each grid cell.

Main point:

- apparent location effects are confounded by where parking is concentrated;
- target abundance and size explain performance better than distance from the centre.

### 4.6 Corrected metrics and OSM comparison

This section will report the limited corrections used to separate model error from reference uncertainty and definitional disagreement.

It will also use the manual reference to assess OSM parking coverage:

- 63.5% of labelled parking area is absent from OSM.
- Some OSM parking polygons appear not to be parking in the imagery, and timestamp checks suggest this cannot simply be described as old data.

This supports the decision not to use OSM as ground truth.

### 4.7 Parking extent and distribution

This section answers RQ3.

| Estimate | Value |
|---|---:|
| Manual parking area | 3.26 km² |
| Share of study area | 3.26% |
| After sampled corrections to the reference | 3.30% |
| Highest distance band | 1-2 km from City Square, 7.1% |
| Median grid-cell share | 1.71% |
| Cells above 10% parking | 6 |

The important result is that surface parking is unevenly distributed. It is not simply highest in the core, but peaks in the 1-2 km ring and is concentrated in a small number of grid cells.

The calibration test will be used to state the scale at which the model output is useful:

- useful for city- or large sub-area estimates after validation;
- unreliable for individual car parks or single grid-cell estimates.

## Chapter 5: Discussion

**Purpose:** interpret the results and state the practical reliability of the model output.

The discussion will answer the RQs directly rather than introduce new results.

**RQ1 answer:** the model transfers enough to identify much of the parking signal, but not enough for uncorrected area measurement. High recall and low precision are the defining pattern.

**RQ2 answer:** the main errors are boundary effects, irregular layouts, land-use confusions and post-processing removals. The model is not simply failing to recognise parking; much of the error comes from edges, definitions and pipeline choices.

**RQ3 answer:** off-street surface parking occupies 3.26% of the 100 km² study area as labelled, or 3.30% after the sampled corrections to the reference. This is a census of the labelling, not a model estimate; the +-7% hold-out figure describes what the method would deliver without labelling and is not an error bar on it. It is spatially concentrated, especially in the 1-2 km band and in a small number of high-parking grid cells.

The discussion will then return to the broader issue of parking as urban land. The result can support aggregate evidence for land-use debates, but it does not identify which specific sites should be redeveloped. That distinction is important for keeping the policy claim proportionate.

**Likely structure**

1. Answering the three RQs.
2. What the transferred map can support.
3. What the transferred map should not be used for.
4. Implications for UK parking evidence and densification debates.
5. Limitations and future work.

**Key limitations to discuss**

- one-city design;
- single-person manual labelling;
- imagery differences between labelling and model input;
- uncertain boundary between parking, private driveways and on-street parking;
- 5 m boundary threshold;
- OSM timestamp limits;
- calibration tested within Leeds but not yet across cities.

## Chapter 6: Conclusion

**Purpose:** close the dissertation by restating the contribution plainly.

The conclusion will summarise the study design, answer the three RQs, and state the contribution:

1. a UK validation of a US-trained surface-parking segmentation model;
2. an error typology separating boundary error, systematic confusion, definitional disagreement and post-processing effects;
3. evidence that the model can support aggregate parking-area estimates after validation, but not uncorrected site-level interpretation;
4. a Leeds estimate of off-street surface parking extent and spatial concentration.

Future work will focus on multi-city validation, UK-specific fine-tuning, better treatment of irregular layouts, possible use of NIR imagery, and more systematic multi-annotator reference data.
